## What review is for

Review catches two different classes of problem, and conflating them makes
reviewers slow and preparers defensive.

**Technical errors** — the return is wrong. Wrong number, wrong treatment,
missing form, failed limitation. These must be fixed.

**Judgment and presentation** — the return is defensible but a choice could be
better, or the presentation invites a question. These are worth raising, but as
discussion rather than correction.

A reviewer who mixes the two produces long note lists where the important items
are buried. Separate them explicitly, and be honest when something is
preference rather than error — preparers learn faster and push back less.

Review is also where a firm's throughput dies. A reviewer who rewrites the
return instead of noting the issue removes the preparer's chance to learn and
guarantees the same note next year. Notes that say what to do and why cost
slightly more to write and far less over a season.

## Universal checks

Regardless of entity type:

- **Prior year comparison.** Large swings in any line, and anything that
  appeared last year but not this year, need an explanation. This single check
  catches more than any other.
- **Carryforwards** actually carried: NOLs, capital losses, charitable
  carryovers, §179 disallowed amounts, passive losses, basis, credits, AMT
  credit. These are lost quietly and are hard to reconstruct later.
- **Elections** attached where required, and consistent with prior years.
- **Estimated payments** agree to the transcript or client records, not just to
  what the client said. Misapplied payments are common.
- **State returns** consistent with federal, with conformity adjustments made.
- **E-file diagnostics** cleared, not just overridden. An override should have a
  workpaper explaining it.
- **Overrides in the software** reviewed specifically — they suppress the
  software's own checks and are a frequent source of quiet error.
- **Preparer and firm information** correct, and the return signed.

## 1040

- **Filing status**, particularly head of household — the support and household
  tests are frequently failed. Verify the qualifying person.
- **Dependents** — relationship, residency, age, support, and whether someone
  else can claim them. Divorced-parent cases need Form 8332.
- **All information returns accounted for.** Compare to the prior year list and,
  where available, to a wage and income transcript. Missing 1099s generate
  automated notices.
- **Brokerage 1099-B** — basis reported vs. not reported, wash sales, and
  whether the consolidated statement was later corrected.
- **Schedule C** — reasonableness of the profit, vehicle and home office
  substantiation, whether the activity is really a business, and whether
  contractors were issued 1099s.
- **Schedule E** — passive loss limitation, at-risk, real estate professional
  status if losses are claimed as active, and correct treatment of improvements
  versus repairs.
- **K-1s** — basis and at-risk limitations applied, correct character carried
  through, state K-1s picked up, QBI information used.
- **Retirement** — excess contributions, early distribution penalty exceptions,
  rollovers correctly reported, required minimum distributions taken.
- **Sale of a residence** — §121 exclusion eligibility and the ownership and use
  tests.
- **Estimated payment and safe harbor** for next year, particularly where income
  jumped.

## 1065 and 1120-S

- **Balance sheet balances**, and the book-to-tax reconciliation makes sense.
- **Capital accounts** roll forward correctly. Partnership capital reporting on
  the tax basis method has specific requirements.
- **Basis computed and tracked per owner** — separate from the capital account.
  For S corporations, confirm any shareholder loans are genuine direct loans if
  debt basis is claimed.
- **Distributions** — proportionate for an S corporation (disproportionate
  distributions risk a second class of stock), and tested against basis.
- **Reasonable compensation** for S corporation shareholder-employees who work
  in the business. If wages are zero and the corporation is profitable, that is
  a finding, not an oversight to let pass.
- **More-than-2% shareholder health insurance** included in W-2 wages.
- **Guaranteed payments** correctly distinguished from distributive share.
- **Separately stated items** actually separated rather than buried in ordinary
  income — the character has to survive to the owner's return.
- **Self-employment income** correctly determined for partners; limited partner
  treatment is fact-dependent and has been litigated.
- **QBI information** reported on the K-1, including wages, qualified property,
  and SSTB status.
- **§754 election** in effect and adjustments computed if there was a transfer
  or a distribution triggering one.
- **Fixed asset additions** reviewed for capitalization vs. repair, and the
  disposal side actually recorded — retired assets left on the schedule are a
  perennial finding.
- **Loans between the entity and owners** documented, with interest.

## 1120

- **Book-to-tax reconciliation** (Schedule M-1 or M-3) complete and supported.
- **Officer compensation** reported on Schedule E of the 1120 series where
  required, and reasonable — the exposure runs the opposite direction from an
  S corporation, since excessive compensation can be recharacterized as a
  dividend.
- **Accumulated earnings** exposure if the corporation is retaining income
  without a documented business purpose.
- **Related-party transactions** at arm's length and disclosed.
- **NOL** carryforward tracked with its limitation.
- **Estimated payments** — corporate rules differ from individual ones.
- **Personal holding company** and **personal service corporation** status
  checked where the facts suggest them.

## 1041

- **Fiduciary accounting income** distinguished from taxable income — they are
  different figures and the distribution deduction runs on the interaction.
- **Distributable net income** computed correctly; it caps the distribution
  deduction and determines the character of what beneficiaries receive.
- **Tier system** applied where there are both required and discretionary
  distributions.
- **§663(b) 65-day election** considered — distributions in the first 65 days
  of the following year can be treated as made in the prior year, a genuinely
  useful planning lever that is easy to miss.
- **Trust instrument** actually read for distribution provisions rather than
  assumed.
- **Compressed rate brackets** — trusts reach the top rate at a very low income
  level, which is why distributing income to beneficiaries is often
  advantageous.
- **Grantor trust status** determined; a grantor trust reports differently.

## Writing review notes

A good note is specific, actionable, and explains why. Compare:

Weak: *"Depreciation looks wrong."*

Better: *"Form 4562 — the Kubota tractor placed in service 6/12 is on a 5-year
life; farm equipment is 7-year property under MACRS. Correct the life and
recompute. Affects both the current deduction and the basis schedule going
forward."*

The second costs thirty extra seconds and eliminates a round trip. It also
teaches, which is the only way a review queue gets shorter over time.

Group notes so the preparer can work efficiently: **must fix**, **discuss**,
and **for next year**. Mixing an open technical question in among formatting
preferences guarantees something gets lost.

## Workpaper standards

A workpaper exists so that someone who was not there — a reviewer, a successor
preparer, an examiner — can reconstruct what was done and why. Write for that
reader.

Each significant workpaper should make clear:

- **Source** of the numbers, specifically enough to find them again.
- **What was done** — the procedure, not just the result.
- **Conclusion**, and the authority for any position that needed one.
- **Preparer and reviewer** sign-off with dates.
- **Open items** carried forward, so they are not silently dropped.

Tie the return to the workpapers and the workpapers to source documents.
Unexplained plugs are the single most common weakness found in practice
inspection.

Note the distinction between **support** and **substantiation**. Support is what
the firm needs to have prepared the return properly. Substantiation is what the
taxpayer must be able to produce on examination — for meals, travel, vehicles,
and charitable contributions the statutory substantiation requirements are
strict, and the firm should confirm the client has it rather than assume.

Retention: firms generally keep workpapers for a period well beyond the normal
assessment statute, and returns longer. Follow the firm's policy, and keep in
mind that the assessment period extends for substantial omissions of income and
does not run at all for a return that was never filed or was fraudulent.

## Disclosure and penalty standards

Positions sit on a ladder of support, and the standard that applies determines
whether disclosure is needed:

- **Reasonable basis** — the lowest standard that avoids a negligence penalty;
  generally requires disclosure to protect against the substantial
  understatement penalty.
- **Substantial authority** — an objective standard, more than reasonable basis;
  generally sufficient without disclosure.
- **More likely than not** — greater than 50% likelihood of success; the
  standard required for certain positions including some tax shelter and
  reportable transaction items.

Disclosure is made on **Form 8275** (or 8275-R for a position contrary to
regulations). A preparer has their own penalty exposure under §6694, distinct
from the taxpayer's, and the applicable standard for the preparer can differ
from the taxpayer's.

When a position is thin, the right conversation with the client is explicit: the
strength of the position, the consequence if it fails, and whether disclosure
changes the calculus. Documenting that conversation protects everyone.

## Responding to IRS notices

Most notices are automated and narrower than they appear. Work them
methodically:

1. **Identify the notice type** — the number in the corner tells you what it is
   and what the deadline is. A CP2000 proposing changes from unmatched
   information returns is very different from a math-error notice or a
   collection notice, and they have different response paths.
2. **Verify before conceding.** A meaningful share of automated notices are
   wrong, or are right about the omission but wrong about the tax — a CP2000
   commonly proposes tax on gross proceeds because basis was never reported.
3. **Meet the deadline**, and note that the response window is often shorter
   than the taxpayer expects. Some deadlines, particularly the 90-day statutory
   notice of deficiency, are jurisdictional and cannot be extended.
4. **Respond in writing**, addressing each proposed change specifically, with
   supporting documentation attached and referenced.
5. **Consider penalty abatement** — first-time abatement for an otherwise
   compliant taxpayer, or reasonable cause where the facts support it.
6. **Get authorization in place** — Form 2848 for representation, or 8821 for
   information access only.

Never advise ignoring a notice, and be careful about amending a return in
response to one when a straightforward explanatory response would resolve it —
amending can restart the clock and complicate the record.
